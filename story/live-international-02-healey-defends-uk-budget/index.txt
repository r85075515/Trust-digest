The budget targets the cost of living amid global economic turmoil, but opposition parties say he has not been bold enough.
What happened
Healey unveiled a package that raises taxes on banks and landlords to fund a £100 winter handout. The Guardian's headline puts the handout at £100 for 20 million households, while the feed text describes 20 million families; the two wordings describe the same £100 payment. Speaking to Bloomberg's Caroline Hepker, Healey said the tax increase on banks would be temporary, and that he had urged the Bank of England to consider lenders' international competitiveness. Gilts held on to their pre-budget gains after what the Financial Times called a modest, uneventful package, and Healey's low-key speech blamed global economic storms for stoking energy prices and the cost-of-living crisis. A Bloomberg digest headline in the same batch also noted a faltering tech rally, with no detail in the snippets.
Why it matters
This budget is Healey's first test of a difficult balance: fund help with the cost of living without spooking the bond market. That the gilt market held its gains suggests the modest package passed that test for now, while the opposition's complaint — not bold enough — shows the political test is the opposite one: whether £100 and a temporary bank tax feel like enough.
Sources agree / disagree
The BBC, Bloomberg, the Financial Times, and The Guardian agree on the core package: a bank and landlord tax rise funding a £100 handout, defended by Healey as his first budget. The apparent numeric disagreement in the feed data is not a conflict: £100 is the handout amount, while 2026 is the budget year, so the figures describe different things.
