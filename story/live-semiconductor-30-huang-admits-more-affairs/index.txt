The board, which stressed the importance of honesty and forthrightness in the meeting, voted the next day to restore him anyway.

What happened: The two previously known relationships were with Alice Huang, the Megaspeed controlling shareholder US officials suspect of working for Chinese intelligence, and — per the New York Times and Reuters — with Nvidia subordinates including healthcare vice president Kimberly Powell. The Journal reports Huang was contrite as he disclosed more. The reinstatement followed a weekend lobbying effort from Washington: per the Journal, President Trump asked Vice President JD Vance to persuade the board, after Vance consulted Attorney General Todd Blanche and others; Trump publicly took credit, telling CNBC it would be "a threat to lose him," and told the New York Times that Huang and his wife Lori have worked out their marital issues. The board's statement cited Huang's candor and input from senior US government levels that his return poses no national security risk. During his 11 days out, the board also formed a National Security and Export Control Committee, and Nvidia has committed to hiring its first chief compliance officer.

Why it matters: The disclosure reframes the reinstatement. The board did not bring Huang back in ignorance of a fuller record — it heard a wider admission of policy-violating relationships with subordinates, on top of an unresolved espionage-adjacent investigation, and still concluded he was indispensable. That choice, made under visible White House pressure, is now the central fact for the congressional hearings in December and for the governance lawsuits already filed.

Sources agree/disagree: Every outlet carrying the admission (TechCrunch, Gizmodo, the New York Post, The Sun, Korea JoongAng Daily) traces it to the same Wall Street Journal reporting and its anonymous sources; there is no second independent account, and neither Nvidia nor Huang has confirmed the number publicly. The surrounding facts — the Sunday meeting, the Monday vote, the Vance lobbying — are consistently reported.

Still uncertain: The exact number and identities beyond the reported "at least four"; whether the Wachtell investigation will publish findings on the additional relationships; whether any involved national-security exposure; and how the board's knowledge affects the December testimony, where Huang is scheduled to face closed-door transcribed interviews on Dec. 1.
