10:T791,Novartis has disclosed nine cases of cancer in patients previously treated with Zolgensma, its gene therapy for spinal muscular atrophy, including two in which there is evidence that the viral vector used to deliver the therapy integrated into the patients' DNA. The disclosure, and a US Food and Drug Administration probe reported alongside it, puts a new question mark over one of the highest-profile gene therapies in medicine.
What happened
Novartis said it has received nine reports of tumours in patients treated with Zolgensma since the therapy was first approved in 2019. In two of the cases, there is evidence that the adeno-associated virus vector used to carry the treatment integrated into patient DNA, a mechanism that has long been a theoretical cancer risk for gene therapies. ET Pharma reports that the FDA is now probing the tumour risk tied to the treatment.
Why it matters
Zolgensma is a one-time treatment for a devastating childhood disease, and its risk-benefit balance has been closely watched since launch. If the vector-integration cases point to a real risk rather than coincidence, regulators and doctors will have to weigh that against a treatment that otherwise transforms outcomes for children with spinal muscular atrophy. The findings also matter beyond one drug: vector integration is a field-wide safety question for gene therapy.
Sources agree / disagree
BioPharma Dive and ET Pharma agree on the core disclosure — nine cases, two with integration evidence. ET Pharma adds the FDA probe. Trust label: Two-source agreement — two outlets agree on the nine cases; the FDA probe is reported by ET Pharma.
Still uncertain
No causal link has been established between Zolgensma and the tumours, and the snippets give no details on tumour types, timing, or the patients' ages.
