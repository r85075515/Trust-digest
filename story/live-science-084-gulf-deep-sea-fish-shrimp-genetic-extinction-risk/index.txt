Published in Nature Communications and highlighted by ScienceDaily, the study found that effective population sizes for all five species fell below the 500-individual threshold conservation biologists use to assess long-term viability, with one lanternfish species losing roughly 8 percent of its genetic diversity over 12 years.

Deep-ocean ecosystems have traditionally been viewed as vast, stable refuges buffered from human impacts. Loss of genetic diversity erodes marine species' resilience to future disturbances such as oil spills, warming waters, and habitat contraction.

ScienceDaily and Earth.com both corroborated the five-species genomic findings and the vulnerability to environmental shocks, with Earth.com detailing the specific effective population thresholds and the 12-year diversity loss in the lanternfish species Diaphus dumerilii.

Researchers noted that because few studies have compared deep-sea populations across adjacent ocean basins, the extent to which migration from neighboring waters can replenish lost genetic diversity remains unknown.
