10:T6d0,The bottleneck in AI has moved from compute to memory. Semiconductor Engineering argues that moving compute closer to memory can cut data movement, power consumption, and latency — and that AI inference, not training, is now reshaping the memory wall.
What happened
The article's key takeaways are that near-memory computing reduces data movement, power, and latency; that different AI workloads need different approaches to memory architecture, interconnects, and processing; and that software support, standardization, and thermal management remain major barriers to wider adoption. AI infrastructure spent recent years racing for compute — more accelerators, larger heterogeneous clusters, higher peak performance — which enabled ever more capable models. But as AI shifts from training in data centers to inference at the edge, infrastructure must support more users, larger models, longer context windows, and more complex applications, where latency and power matter as much as raw performance.
Why it matters
Inference is where AI actually reaches users, and its cost and latency decide whether applications can scale. The companies and standards that solve the memory wall first will set the terms for the next generation of AI infrastructure, which is why this debate matters beyond chip engineering.
