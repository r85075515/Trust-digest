10:T5b9,What happened
In findings published on November 26, 2026, researchers associated with the American Museum of Natural History reanalyzed a well-known 345-million-year-old fossil specimen using high-resolution X-ray imaging. Beneath the visible rock surface, the scan revealed preserved internal gills and thousands of tiny anatomical features, demonstrating that the organism belongs to a completely different group than scientists had assumed for decades.

Why it matters
Early Carboniferous fossils from roughly 345 million years ago preserve critical evolutionary transitions among early aquatic and vertebrate lineages. Showing that classic specimens can harbor concealed soft-tissue or fine skeletal structures underscores how modern imaging tools can correct foundational errors in evolutionary trees.

Source comparison
ScienceDaily and Dino Science Center both reported on the November 26 release detailing the American Museum of Natural History study. Both reports aligned on the 345-million-year age of the specimen and the discovery of hidden gills and thousands of tiny structures via X-ray imaging.

What remains uncertain
Full taxonomic reassignment details and how broadly the revised classification shifts neighboring fossil groupings await broader peer review and follow-up specimen scans.
