Burnham said Britain must face up to the past, in what the New York Times called a major change in British policy.
What happened
Burnham told Sky News and the BBC he is open to apology calls, while telling the BBC reparations are not the right answer. The remarks came as King Charles continues a Caribbean tour. The Guardian reports planned protests in St John’s, Antigua’s capital, where demonstrators will greet the King and Burnham with billboards, placards, and T-shirts carrying the message: The royal family enslaved us! Apologise now! Reparatory justice now! The Guardian says activists see the Commonwealth leaders’ summit in Antigua next week as an opportunity to demand that Britain confront its role in transatlantic enslavement.
Why it matters
The split verdict is the point: an apology acknowledges moral responsibility, while rejecting reparations refuses financial redress, and that gap is exactly what protesters are targeting. Burnham is testing whether symbolic acknowledgment can ease Commonwealth pressure without opening a reparations claim; the protests show openness alone has not satisfied the demand.
Sources agree / disagree
The New York Times, Sky News, the BBC, and the Guardian all cover the same remarks and tour, agreeing that Burnham is open to an apology and opposed to reparations, with no conflicting facts in the material supplied. The Guardian adds protest plans; the New York Times stresses the change is unlikely to satisfy reparations advocates. Trust label: Multi-source agreement — four outlets agree on the core claims about Burnham’s position and the Caribbean tour.
Still uncertain
