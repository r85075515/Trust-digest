10:Ta06,Lede: Four days after Jensen Huang's resignation, the scandal reached the rulebook. On Monday the Commerce Department moved to shut the remote-access route that let Chinese firms rent America's best AI chips instead of buying them.

What happened: The Bureau of Industry and Security issued a 132-page interim final rule restricting remote access to advanced AI chips and servers. Overseas data centers that supply significant volumes of computing power to firms headquartered in high-risk countries — a group including China, Russia and Iran — are brought under control, with existing providers given 90 days to obtain a license, according to the rule text. The rule also adds a license requirement for advanced AI chip shipments to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil, countries Washington sees as diversion risks. BIS said the rule is "designed to ensure that existing restrictions on access to sensitive American technology cannot be circumvented through remote computing arrangements involving data centers in third countries."

Why it matters: This is the first ban-level policy response to the Nvidia/Megaspeed scandal, extending the principle from "you cannot sell the chips" to "you cannot sell the compute either." The release took the unusual step of noting the rule was "written by and at the direction of the Department of Treasury and the National Security Council" — a public sign that Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and the NSC pushed it through over a Commerce Department they accused of dragging its feet on Megaspeed, raising the pressure on Secretary Howard Lutnick again. The timing is pointed: days before President Trump's trip to China to meet Xi Jinping, and four days after Huang stepped down.

Sources agree / disagree: The Wall Street Journal, The New York Times and ZeroHedge agree on the rule's scope and the five-country license list. The 90-day licensing window comes from ZeroHedge's reading of the rule text. The New York Post separately reported that Trump defended Lutnick on Monday, saying the delay on Megaspeed made sense to avoid a blowup with China.

Still uncertain: The rule's exact effective dates and licensing standards, the practical impact on existing Southeast Asian data-center contracts, whether Beijing retaliates (US officials say they do not expect countermeasures), and whether Megaspeed itself lands on the Entity List all remain open.
