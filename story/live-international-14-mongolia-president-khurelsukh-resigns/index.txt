10:Tbdd,Mongolia's President Khurelsukh Ukhnaa announced on Tuesday that he will resign, three days after a senior figure from a faction of his own ruling party accused him of taking a $25 million kickback from a state railway project built while he was prime minister. In a televised address to the nation, Khurelsukh denied any wrongdoing. "I have never stolen from my people or my state," he said. "I will hand over my duties as president and become an ordinary citizen," adding that he would cooperate with investigators and seek to restore his honour, and pursue legal action against those he says defamed him.

What happened: The accusation came on Saturday from Nyambaatar Khishgee, a former mayor of Ulaanbaatar and former justice minister, who said $110 million tied to the Tavantolgoi-Gashuunsukhait coal railway to the Chinese border was paid out as kickbacks to politicians, $25 million of it to Khurelsukh. The opposition Democratic Party then demanded that every official named step down and pushed toward impeachment. Two other officials named in the claims — Deputy Justice Minister Munkh-Erdene Dembereltseren and Cabinet secretariat head Enkhbayar Battumur — also said Tuesday they will resign. The resignation is not yet final: under Mongolia's Law on the Presidency, the 126-seat parliament, the State Great Khural, must accept it. A vote is expected this week (reported for November 19); if accepted, Speaker Sandag Byambatsogt becomes acting president and a presidential election must follow within four months. Khurelsukh, president since June 2021 with a term running to 2027, would be the first Mongolian president to leave office this way.

Why it matters: A sitting head of state is stepping down over a corruption allegation — unproven and denied — after months of turmoil inside the ruling Mongolian People's Party, which has already cycled through three prime ministers this year. The executive branch otherwise stays intact: Prime Minister Nyam-Osoryn Uchral's cabinet remains and the 2027 budget passed last week is unaffected, so the immediate question is political credibility, not a government collapse.

Sources agree / disagree: AP and the Reuters-sourced reporting agree on the accusation, the $25 million figure, the denial and the parliamentary procedure. Reports differ on framing of the accuser — an early AP version described him as opposition, later corrected to a faction figure within the ruling party; the $110 million total kickback figure comes from the accuser via Mongolian reporting and has not been independently verified.

Still uncertain: No evidence for the kickback claim has been made public; investigators have announced no findings; parliament has not yet voted. Whether the accusation is substantiated, and who contests the presidential election, are both open.
