What happened
Voters delivered a sweeping rebuke of President Trump and his party in the midterms, handing Democrats both chambers of Congress and dramatically reshaping the balance of power in Washington. Trump now enters a lame-duck phase of his second term, facing the largest Democratic majorities in decades, promising both to use his veto power and to work across the aisle. Markets reacted fast: US stocks surged and the dollar slid as investors digested the Democratic victories, lifted by hopes that Trump's turbulent policymaking would be restrained, while government bond yields fell. Trump has conceded that the Iran war cost Republicans dearly in the midterms, and now must decide whether to cut his losses and reach a deal or double down and return to bombing. Burnham, quoted in The Guardian's UK politics live blog, framed the outcome as an opening for the world to turn to living costs and to ending the war.
Why it matters
The verdict is simple: a flipped Congress restrains Trump at home and resets every capital's calculus abroad. For Kyiv, Moscow, Tehran, and Tel Aviv, the question is no longer what Trump alone will do, but what a Democratic Congress will allow, fund, or block. For markets, the rally is a bet that divided government means fewer shocks. The Iran decision is the sharpest test: striking again would be deeply unpopular and keep gas prices high, while a deal would mean admitting the war cost his party the election.

NPR, The Guardian, PBS NewsHour, Axios, the Financial Times, Al Jazeera, Grist, and MIT Technology Review all cover the same core result — Democrats swept Congress — from different angles: markets, the Iran war, climate races, and energy.
