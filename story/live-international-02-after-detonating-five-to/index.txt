The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) reported zero airborne lead after the blast, and officials lifted the shelter-in-place order in Middletown, Iowa.

What happened
The Army said the detonation was necessary to prevent the possible spontaneous explosion of a stockpile of material used in igniters and blasting caps, and carried it out after a federal court gave the green light. Debris flew into the air as several tons of the highly volatile compound were destroyed on the plant grounds. Six federal agencies issued a joint statement after the blast, including the Army, the Department of Health and Human Services, the EPA and the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives. Officials called the operation a success but took no questions from reporters. The BBC, The Verge, PBS NewsHour, The Hill and Axios all reported the blast and the lifted shelter order. Surrounding communities remain on alert, and three states say they are monitoring the effects.

Why it matters
The Army chose a calculated risk: blow up an unstable stockpile under controlled conditions rather than let it sit and possibly detonate on its own. The EPA's zero airborne lead reading is the first hard evidence the gamble paid off, but it is a single early measurement, not a clean bill of health. The decision to take no questions leaves residents to weigh official reassurance against their own worry.
