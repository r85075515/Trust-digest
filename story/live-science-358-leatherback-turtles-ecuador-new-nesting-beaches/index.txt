10:T763,What happened
Researchers from Simon Fraser University (SFU) reported that critically endangered Eastern Pacific leatherback sea turtles are establishing new nesting grounds along several beaches in Ecuador during the 2025–2026 nesting season as ocean temperatures warm. Although Ecuador was not historically recognized as a major nesting area for the subspecies, conservationists documented rising nesting activity, including a tagged female named Bella that nested four to five times in 2026, producing 134 hatchlings from 146 eggs for a 91.8 percent emergence success rate.

Why it matters
Eastern Pacific leatherbacks—the world's largest sea turtle subspecies—have been projected to face extinction within 60 years due to climate change, coastal development, and fisheries bycatch. Evidence that females are shifting southward to cooler or newly suitable Ecuadorian beaches offers rare ecological resilience and informs community-led coastal hatchery management.

Source differences
Simon Fraser University's official release, "Endangered leatherbacks finding new nesting beaches in Ecuador as Pacific Ocean warms, study finds", provides detailed field observations from Manabí province, quotes adjunct professor Juan José Alava, and recounts the multi-clutch tracking data for the tagged turtle Bella. ScienceDaily's summary, "Facing extinction, the world's largest sea turtle is making a surprising move", distills the same SFU research announcement into a brief overview of the 2025–2026 Ecuadorian beach findings.

Still uncertain
Researchers have not yet determined how many total breeding females are shifting to Ecuadorian coastlines each season or whether warming trends will eventually threaten the sand temperatures of these newly colonized beaches.
