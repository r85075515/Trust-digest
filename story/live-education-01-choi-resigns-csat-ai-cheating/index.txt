10:Te27,South Korea's Education Minister Choi Kyo-jin resigned on Sunday, taking responsibility for the nationwide confusion caused by anti-cheating measures for the national college entrance exam that kept changing until days before the test, amid spreading fears of AI-assisted cheating. President Lee Jae Myung accepted the resignation within about an hour of Choi offering it.

What happened: Choi offered his resignation at around 11:30 a.m. on Sunday, saying that changes made with the College Scholastic Ability Test (CSAT) just ahead had inconvenienced test-takers and the public, and that he keenly felt responsible now that the exam was over. Chief presidential spokesperson Kang Yu-jung said Lee was briefed and accepted the offer the same day; a successor will be selected through due procedure. The crisis built after the top scorer on the certified tax accountant exam admitted on Nov. 15 to cheating with a miniature camera and AI. The Education Ministry, which had initially focused on smart glasses, rushed out added measures on Nov. 17 (stickers over clothing buttons, tighter eyeglass inspections) and again on Nov. 18 (limiting writing implements to black pencils). On exam day, Thursday, authorities blocked public Wi-Fi around test centers and caught 111 suspected cases of cheating with electronic devices. Choi had already drawn fire at a Nov. 16 National Assembly hearing, where, asked whether he could guarantee detecting all AI-enabled cheating, he replied that he did not think he could, citing his age; he was then largely absent from ministry events, with Vice Minister Choi Eun-ok appearing in his place. Lee wrote on X after accepting the resignation that concerns over AI-exploiting cheating are spreading beyond the CSAT to essay-based admissions and school tests, and that exam fairness is a principle that can never be compromised. Korea JoongAng Daily also reported that on exam day a perfect scorer on the Legal Education Eligibility Test who had been accused of using AI to cheat was found dead at a Seoul motel.

Why it matters: This is, by multiple accounts, the first time AI-assisted cheating concerns have directly cost a cabinet minister his job. The CSAT is a once-a-year, society-stopping exam in South Korea, and the episode shows consumer AI and micro-cameras outrunning the state's exam-security playbook — a governance problem every country running high-stakes standardized tests now faces. Formally a voluntary resignation, it is widely read in Seoul as an effective dismissal.

Sources agree/disagree: Korea JoongAng Daily, the Seoul Economic Daily, TBS News (citing The Guardian) and AFP-aligned coverage agree on the resignation, its acceptance within about an hour, the Nov. 15 tax-accountant-exam confession as the catalyst, the sequence of last-minute measures and the 111 suspected cases. Outlets differ only in emphasis: the Seoul Economic Daily stresses the punitive, effectively-dismissal reading, while Korea JoongAng Daily foregrounds Choi's own statement and the president's X post.

Still uncertain: No successor has been named, and it is not yet clear what permanent anti-cheating regime — device rules, inspection powers, penalties — will govern next year's CSAT. Whether the 111 suspected cases will be confirmed as AI-assisted cheating, and whether further officials will be held responsible, also remains open.
