10:T904,Neurologist Wang Han-cheng, who translated an American book on voluntarily stopping eating and drinking, questions some of Taiwan's current end-of-life fasting practices. He argues that acknowledging a patient's wish to end life sooner does not mean clinicians can directly take part.
What happened
Wang translated the American book on voluntary fasting, yet does not endorse some of the practices now described in Taiwan as fasting to hasten death. His position, as reported by The Reporter, turns on three requirements: a professional assessment of the patient's decision-making capacity, the necessary conditions of medical care, and the legal risks clinicians may face. In his view, Taiwan still lacks the full discussion and safeguards those requirements demand. A second item from the same outlet poses the questions behind his concern: when a patient says, I do not want to eat anymore, how can doctors confirm the choice is made with full understanding of the consequences and free from pressure? If the patient can no longer express a wish, can family members decide instead? Doctors and legal scholars are examining how to identify a genuinely autonomous wish, from fasting choices among older people to feeding disputes involving people who have lost capacity.
Why it matters
The distinction Wang draws is the whole argument: recognizing that a patient wants to die sooner is not the same as helping bring death about. That line is where decision-making capacity, care, and the law meet, and it is why he says Taiwan needs discussion and protection before practice outruns them.
Sources agree / disagree
Both items come from a single outlet, The Reporter, so the second item does not independently corroborate the first. The two items present one line of reporting: Wang's critique, followed by the questions doctors and legal scholars are asking about it. Trust label: Single source — one outlet, The Reporter; the critique is Wang's position, not a corroborated finding.
Still uncertain
The snippets do not name the American book's author, do not cite any law or regulation, and do not describe any specific case.
