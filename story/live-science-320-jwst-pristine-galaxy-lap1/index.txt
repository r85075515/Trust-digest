10:T72b,What happened
An international team led by Associate Professor Kimihiko Nakajima (Kimihiko Nakajima) of Kanazawa University used NASA's James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) to observe a small, distant galaxy designated LAP1 as it existed roughly 13 billion years ago. Spectroscopic measurements revealed an extraordinarily metal-poor chemical composition—nearly devoid of elements heavier than hydrogen and helium—providing strong observational evidence for pristine star formation in the early universe.

Why it matters
In cosmology, the universe's very first stars, known as Population III stars, formed solely from primordial hydrogen and helium before synthesizing heavier elements that seeded later generations of stars and planets. Identifying a nearly unpolluted galaxy like LAP1 offers a rare observational window into how the first luminous structures assembled out of pristine gas.

Source differences
Kanazawa University's research feature ("The Most Chemically Primitive Galaxy in the Early Universe - 金沢大学") and ScienceDaily ("James Webb discovers a nearly pristine galaxy from 13 billion years") detail Associate Professor Kimihiko Nakajima's team's findings on LAP1's chemical primitiveness and its alignment with early-universe star formation models. SciTechDaily ("James Webb Discovers a Nearly Pristine Galaxy From the Dawn of") elaborates on the spectroscopic signatures linking LAP1 to theoretical Population III star clusters.

Still uncertain
Astronomers note that definitive confirmation of individual Population III stars inside LAP1 requires deeper spectroscopic follow-up to rule out trace enrichment from earlier unseen stellar explosions.
