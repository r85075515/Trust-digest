10:T9df,Nvidia has stopped shipping its licensed Hopper data-center products to China, interim chief executive Colette Kress told analysts on Tuesday's earnings call — ending the H200 sales arrangement that Jensen Huang brokered with President Donald Trump. "Our guidance does not have any licensed Hopper data center products included, and we are no longer shipping licensed Hopper data center products to China," Kress said. The company sold $2.4 billion of the licensed Hopper products to China in the third quarter, roughly 2% of data center revenue.

What happened: The arrangement being wound down was the exception Huang fought for: H200 exports to approved Chinese customers were allowed provided the chips were routed through the United States, with 25% paid to the U.S. government. Beijing had in any case largely refused to accept H200 deliveries. Nvidia did not explain why shipments stopped and did not tie the decision to the Commerce Department's November 16 rule barring advanced chips from overseas data centers serving Chinese customers. The disclosure came the same day the board announced its new National Security and Export Control Oversight Committee, and Nvidia's fourth-quarter guidance assumes no data center compute revenue from China at all.

Why it matters: This is the new leadership's clearest break with the Huang era. For years Huang argued that keeping China inside Nvidia's ecosystem served U.S. competitiveness; within a week of his resignation, his signature China arrangement is dead, the board has a standing national-security committee, and the company is voluntarily exiting a licensed market it had lobbied to keep. Investment bank Jefferies estimates the November 16 rules could trim $44 billion to $62 billion from Nvidia's data center revenue next year; Kress countered that the backlog for newer chips such as Vera Rubin means no impact on top-line revenue.

Sources agree / disagree: Bloomberg, TechCrunch, Gizmodo and others report the same Kress remarks; no source disputes the halt. Reports differ only in framing — whether the stop is best read as compliance-driven or as a leadership signal — and Nvidia itself has given no reason.

Still uncertain: Why Nvidia stopped (the company has not said), whether shipments could resume under a future CEO or a changed policy, and how much of the Jefferies-estimated exposure materializes.
