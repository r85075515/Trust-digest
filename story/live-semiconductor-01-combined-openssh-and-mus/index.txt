10:T7ab,A combined flaw in OpenSSH and the musl C library lets an attacker gain root access on certain Linux systems simply by typing a password longer than 256 characters, Tom's Hardware and The Register report, and emergency fixes have been issued for millions of systems.
What happened
The bug, dubbed *sshole by the Wakefield researcher who discovered it, Özgün Kültekin, can only be exploited under very specific conditions. It affects certain Linux systems that use musl libc instead of the traditional GNU C Library, and only those running OpenSSH. On those systems, entering a password longer than 256 characters at the login prompt can hand over root access without a valid password. Affected distributions include Alpine Linux and OpenWRT, according to Tom's Hardware.
Why it matters
The flaw turns the most ordinary security step — typing a password — into the attack itself, and it hits distributions including Alpine Linux and OpenWRT. Emergency fixes for OpenSSH and musl libc have been issued, and the coverage stresses patching immediately. Any delay leaves systems that match the narrow conditions exposed to trivial compromise.
Sources agree / disagree
Tom's Hardware and The Register agree on the core claim: a password longer than 256 characters can yield root on affected musl-based systems running OpenSSH. The ingest system flagged a 512-versus-257 figure difference, but neither figure appears as a conflicting claim in the snippets provided, so it is not treated as a disagreement here. Trust label: Two-source agreement — two outlets agree on the flaw and its trigger.
Still uncertain
The snippets do not list every affected distribution or version, give no details of how the fix should be applied beyond patching promptly, and do not say how widely the flaw has been exploited, if at all.
