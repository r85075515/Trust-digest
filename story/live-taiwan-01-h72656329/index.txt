Two official responses in the feed text pointed in different directions: a ruling-party legislator spoke of better treatment on humanitarian grounds, while the Mainland Affairs Council said the man would be sent back to Thailand under existing mechanisms.
What happened
CNA reported that Cheng flew from Thailand and arrived on November 2, recorded a video that he posted online asking for help, and sought short-term humanitarian protection. A second CNA item said he had left the aircraft at Taoyuan Airport and asked the government for temporary detention. Democratic Progressive Party legislative caucus official Chuang Jui-hsiung said that, from the standpoint of humanitarianism and human rights, better treatment could indeed be given, and that he believed government departments would think it through carefully. The Mainland Affairs Council responded the same day that the government could not verify the claimed persecution, that similar cases are handled on an established basis for consideration, and that at present Cheng could only be sent back to Thailand under the existing mechanism.
Why it matters
The case matters because it tests how Taiwan handles an individual, unverified protection claim at the border, balancing a humanitarian argument made in the legislature against an executive agency statement that the claim cannot be verified. The two CNA items together show both sides of that handling in one day: a legislator urging careful thought and better treatment, and the council stating the operational outcome under current rules. The feed text does not include Cheng's own account in detail, any evidence for the persecution claim, or any statement from Thailand, so the underlying claim itself cannot be judged from this material. What can be reported is the sequence of arrival, request and the two responses as CNA presented them.
