10:T908,A single day without food can switch on a repair response in the gut, scientists have found: fasting for just 24 hours increases a beneficial gut bacterium that helps the intestines regenerate after radiation damage. The finding is preclinical, but the researchers say it could pave the way for treatments that protect healthy tissue during cancer therapy — potentially without requiring patients to fast at all. What happened Scientists discovered that a 24-hour fast boosts levels of a beneficial gut bacterium, and that the bacterium helps the intestines regrow after radiation damage. The work is preclinical — tested before human trials — and the proposed payoff is practical: new treatments that shield healthy tissue while cancer patients undergo radiation therapy. The researchers suggest the protective effect might eventually be delivered without fasting itself, which would matter for patients too weak to go without food. ScienceDaily's November 8 item reports the finding; the feed snippet does not name the bacterium or the research institution. Why it matters The verdict is that the finding matters most as a shortcut: if a fasting-triggered bacterium can be isolated or mimicked, cancer patients could get the gut-protecting benefit of a fast without enduring one. Radiation damage to the intestines is a dose-limiting problem in cancer care — it caps how much treatment a patient can receive — so anything that helps the gut regenerate could let doctors treat more aggressively while doing less collateral harm. Sources agree / disagree Only one source covers the finding, a ScienceDaily health and medicine item, so there is no independent corroboration. The item is internally consistent, and its limits are stated plainly: the work is preclinical, and the treatment idea is a possibility, not a proven therapy. Trust label: Single source — one ScienceDaily item only; treat the finding as early-stage and unconfirmed. Still uncertain The feed snippet leaves the biggest questions open: which bacterium is involved, how much it increases, what animal or model the preclinical work used, and whether the effect has been shown in people.
