10:Taf6,What happened
In a study published in Nature on November 26, 2026 (DOI: 10.1038/s41586-026-11341-6), researchers led by first author Xing Song (Song Xing) and corresponding author Qiaomei Fu of the Institute of Vertebrate Paleontology and Paleoanthropology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, analyzed four fossil hominin teeth excavated from two caves in Guangxi, southern China. Two premolars from Chuanshandong in Long'an County, Nanning, date to at least about 100,800 years ago, while two teeth from Longchuandong in Luzhai County, Liuzhou, date to roughly 104,500 and 42,600 years ago. Combining morphological assessment with palaeoproteomic analysis—which successfully recovered enamel proteins from three teeth and identified all three individuals as male—the team assigned the Longchuandong individuals to a Denisovan-related group and classified the Chuanshandong remains as an unresolved archaic Homo lineage.

Why it matters
The Longchuandong fossils provide the first direct evidence that Denisovan populations inhabited the subtropical Lingnan region of southern China, substantially expanding their known geographic and ecological range from cold high-altitude and northern environments into warm southern latitudes. Meanwhile, the unresolved Chuanshandong lineage suggests that distinct, primitive hominin groups persisted into the Late Pleistocene alongside Denisovans and early modern humans in East Asia.

Source differences
The primary research article in Nature, titled "Middle–Late Pleistocene Denisovans and unresolved Homo in southern China," uses the transliterated cave names Chuanshandong and Longchuandong and details the morphological and enamel palaeoproteomic evidence. Phys.org published a research summary report on November 27 titled "Denisovan remains and an unknown archaic human lineage discovered in southern China," synthesizing the paper's dating and lineage assignments. People's Daily Online carried a Xinhua report on November 26 titled "Fossils extend Denisovan range into subtropical south China, reveal unknown human lineage," which translates the two sites into English as Chuanshan Cave and Longchuan Cave (referring to the same localities as Chuanshandong and Longchuandong) and includes direct reporting on the research team's findings.

Still uncertain
Because ancient DNA recovery remains challenging in warm, humid subtropical cave environments, the exact phylogenetic placement of the unresolved Chuanshandong archaic Homo lineage—and whether it interbred with Denisovans or early Homo sapiens—remains open pending further fossil or biomolecular discoveries.
