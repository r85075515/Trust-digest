What happened
According to the incident account summarized in the feeds, the attackers tricked a Binance software engineer into downloading malware onto a work-issued computer during a fake job interview process. The exchange published an incident report on Tuesday saying several techniques were used, including social engineering via fake job interviews and malware downloads, and the compromise preceded last week's record-breaking theft. United States government agencies and cyber analysts have blamed Pyongyang for the heist, the feeds say. The case is therefore presented not as a sudden technical failure alone, but as the culmination of a long-running campaign that researchers had already documented and warned about.
Why it matters
The story matters because the method succeeded against a very large exchange despite advance warnings, which raises questions about hiring-process security, work-device controls, and how warnings are turned into defenses. A fake interview is an ordinary business interaction, so the attack path exploits trust rather than only code. If a single engineer's work computer can become an entry point, the reports imply that screening, device isolation, and verification during recruitment carry direct financial risk for crypto firms holding very large sums.
Sources agree / disagree
TechCrunch and The Register both frame the theft as a North Korean operation using a fake job interview, while BBC Technology headlines North Korea's denial that it was behind the hack. Trust label: Accounts differ — the outlets agree on the scale and the reported method, but they differ on attribution because one headline centers the denial.
